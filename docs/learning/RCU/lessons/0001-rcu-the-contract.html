0001 · The RCU contract and the zombie list

One lesson, two things: the sequence of steps that makes read-copy-update correct, and the one data structure that makes its memory reclamation terminate. Everything here is anchored to src/qor/essentials/datastructures/. Print it, or read it on screen; the quiz below needs a browser.

What this lesson gets you. By the end you should be able to say, without looking: the seven steps of the contract; which of them a reader performs; why a reader never touches a mutex; what a zombie list entry is and why one list holds two kinds of entry; and why the reclaim scan walks only toward older registrations.

Companions: the reference sheet · the glossary. Use the glossary’s words in your answers - that vocabulary is the point.

Part A · The knowledge (read this once, then hide it)

A.1 The problem RCU is solving

Some threads iterate a pointer-linked collection very often; a few mutate it rarely. A normal mutex makes the common case pay for the rare one. RCU makes the readers pay almost nothing and moves the cost onto the writer - and onto whichever reader finishes first.

The contract that buys this: an updater may never block a reader and may never force one to retry (Wikipedia). A reader therefore cannot wait, and cannot copy the whole structure. The only lever left is memory lifetime: a reader must never be handed a freed pointer.

In this repo, a reader is literally two lines. From signalbase.cpp:128-131:

auto senderListHandle = m_connectList.lock_read();   for (auto& item : *senderListHandle) { ... }   // m_connectList is SharedList<ConnectStruct> - signalbase.h:105

Everything RCU does is invisible underneath those two lines. That is the lesson: the machinery is in the destructor of senderListHandle, not in the loop.

A.2 The contract, in the order it runs

1. Register - announce that you may be readingreader, writer rculist.h:184-195, reached via rcuguarded.h:74-81 / 122-129

5. Unlink - splice the old node out, atomicallywriter rculist.h:589-617
6. Retire - hand the old node to the zombie list, do not free itwriter rculist.h:618-626

Steps 3-6 are writer-only. A reader does only 1, 2 and 7. That is why reads are cheap: three actions, one of them a compare-and-swap, none of them a mutex.

A.3 The four things that must stay true

I1 · Readers never block and never retry. No mutex, no CAS loop, no sleep on the read path. The write mutex lives inside the list (rculist.h:156) and readers never open it.

I2 · A walk is never torn. Both links are std::atomic<node*> (rculist.h:122-123), so every hop is one atomic store. A reader may see the old chain, or the new one, and may straddle an update from hop to hop - never a half-updated node.

I3 · Retired memory outlives every reader that could have seen it. This is what the header means by “iterators are never invalidated by any list operation” (rculist.h:35-37). An iterator is a bare node* (rculist.h:328): its safety comes from the zombie list, not from ownership.

I4 · One writer at a time. RCU is not a concurrent-write structure. Every mutator takes std::lock_guard<M> - rculist.h:503, 524, 545, 567, 588. Writers pay a mutex; readers pay nothing.

A.4 The zombie list - the one structure worth drawing

One lock-free stack, newest entry at the head, holding two interleaved kinds of entry. Both are pushed by the same CAS shape (rculist.h:191-194 in rcu_read_lock, 623-626 in erase).

m_zombie_head -> [guard C] -> [dead B] -> [guard A] -> nullptr newest owner!=0 owner==0 owner!=0 zombie=0 zombie=B zombie=0

Guard entry (ctor rculist.h:132): owner points at the live guard; zombie_node is null. Dead-node entry (ctor 130): zombie_node is the unlinked node; owner is null, because it is not a reader.

The unregister scan - rcu_guard::unlock(), rculist.h:206-245 - is two passes over entries older than this guard:

  • Probe (208-220): walk from m_zombie->next looking for any entry with owner != nullptr. One found means a reader is still out there, so nothing older than it may be freed.
  • Free (224-243): only if the probe found none, destroy and deallocate every entry walked - the dead node and its zombie_list_node wrapper - then splice this guard past the survivors (line 242).
  • Release (244): m_zombie->owner.store(nullptr). This guard is now itself reclaimable, by some older guard’s scan.

Why only older entries? A newer registration began after this one finished, so it cannot be holding a pointer this one obtained. The only registration whose reach is unknown is the one running the scan. Reclamation is thus amortised onto whichever reader finishes first - which is why this userspace code needs no global sync point of any kind.

A.5 Why a writer registers too

Read rculist.h:250: rcu_write_lock is one line, rcu_read_lock(list). It is not an oversight. erase() takes a const_iterator - the writer walks the chain with the same machinery a reader uses, so it too can be standing on a node another writer just retired. Registering is what pins that. A writer is a reader that also holds the write mutex.

Part C · Making it stick, and what comes next

Spacing. This quiz is one page of practice. Do not re-read Part A to beat a score - close the tab today, and in two or three days open only the reference sheet and re-answer these seven questions from memory. A second session that finds you still at 7/7 means the model is durable; a session where you have to look it up means it was fluency, not storage.

Interleaving. Next session, mix this with a different but related unit - the atomic-linking rules of a doubly-linked list under a single writer, or the write-mutex discipline elsewhere in QOR. Retrieval practice works better when related topics compete for the same memory.

Wisdom. This model is worth testing against practitioners once you can recite it: the CopperSpice forum maintains the upstream of this exact code, and r/cpp will challenge the design. Ask there whether a per-list guard chain is still the right shape for a list that is iterated but almost never mutated.
